Abstract —
Modules 1 and 2 of CalmSpace address emotion detection and real-time therapeutic response during structured sessions. However, a fundamental gap remains: the average therapy session for an autistic child lasts 45-60 minutes. The child then spends the remaining 22+ hours of the day in environments — home, school, transit — where no adaptive support is active, no emotional data is recorded, and no caregiver has visibility. Module 3, titled Beyond the Session, addresses this gap with two tightly sequenced subsystems. F3.1, the Daily Life Continuity Engine (DLCE), establishes a lightweight home check-in ritual and passive logging layer that extends CalmSpace coverage into daily life, generating a weekly home report for caregivers. F3.2, the Predictive Crisis Prevention System (PCPS), consumes the longitudinal data produced by F3.1 and Module 2 sessions to train a personalised ML model per child, capable of issuing proactive alerts before a crisis threshold is reached. Together, these subsystems represent the transition of CalmSpace from a reactive therapy tool to a proactive, always-aware support platform.

I. INTRODUCTION — THE 23-HOUR PROBLEM

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition characterised by differences in social communication, sensory processing, and behavioural regulation. The global prevalence is estimated at 1 in 100 children (WHO, 2023), with 1 in 36 in the United States alone (CDC, 2023). Evidence-based interventions — Applied Behaviour Analysis (ABA), speech therapy, occupational therapy — are effective when consistently applied. However, their impact is severely constrained by a structural reality: formal therapy occupies, at most, 5-10 hours per week. The remaining 158 hours belong to an unsupported, unmonitored daily life.

This is what we call the 23-Hour Problem. For every one hour that CalmSpace's Module 2 actively supports an autistic child during therapy, there are twenty-three hours where no adaptive system is watching, no emotional data is being collected, and no caregiver has real-time visibility. A meltdown at 9am on a Tuesday is not random. It is almost always the product of accumulated stressors — a poor sleep the night before, a noisy bus ride, an unexpected schedule change. But without data from those preceding hours, it is invisible to the therapist and the parent.

KEY INSIGHT
Module 3 does not add more therapy. It adds continuity. It ensures that the data generated during therapy sessions in Module 2 is connected to the data from the child's daily life — so that the full picture is always visible, and the next crisis can be predicted before it happens.

Module 3 comprises two sequential subsystems. F3.1, the Daily Life Continuity Engine (DLCE), solves the data gap by establishing a non-intrusive home check-in ritual and passive emotional logging layer. F3.2, the Predictive Crisis Prevention System (PCPS), solves the insight gap by training a personalised ML model on the accumulated longitudinal data to issue proactive alerts to caregivers and teachers before a crisis threshold is reached.

These are not independent features. F3.1 is the data collection infrastructure that makes F3.2 possible. F3.2 is the payoff that makes F3.1 worth doing. They are designed as a pipeline, not a menu.

III. F3.1 — DAILY LIFE CONTINUITY ENGINE (DLCE)

A) Motivation and Problem Statement
Research by Strauss et al. (2012) demonstrates that parental involvement and home-based data collection are among the strongest predictors of therapy outcome improvement in ASD. Yet the standard information channel between therapy and home is a brief verbal exchange or a handwritten note. Critical data — what happened between sessions, what the child's emotional baseline has been, what home triggers occurred — is routinely lost.

The DLCE is designed to close this channel. It does not ask parents to become data analysts. It does not ask children to do extra work. It establishes a 5-minute evening check-in ritual that feels like a simple conversation with the bot — and quietly logs everything needed for the prediction system and the weekly home report.

B) The Evening Check-in — How It Works
Each evening, at a time set by the caregiver (default: 7:30pm), the CalmSpace app sends a gentle notification. The child opens the app and is greeted by the conversational companion from Module 2 — already a familiar presence. The interaction is deliberately short, structured, and predictable:

EXAMPLE CHECK-IN
Bot: 'Hi! How was today? Pick one.' [Icons: Good day / Okay day / Hard day] Child selects 'Hard day.' Bot: 'Thanks for telling me. What made it hard?' [Icons: Too noisy / Someone was unkind / Felt worried / Too many changes / Other] Child selects 'Too noisy.' Bot: 'That sounds really tough. You handled it. Want to do a quick breathing exercise before bed?' Session logged. Parent notified: 'Today logged as Hard Day. Trigger: noise sensitivity.'

The interaction is icon-first, with text as secondary option. This is consistent with AAC research showing that icon-based interfaces reduce response latency and anxiety in autistic users by up to 38% compared to text-only interfaces (Beukelman & Mirenda, 2013). The entire check-in is designed to take under 3 minutes.

D) Weekly Home Report
Every Sunday evening, DLCE automatically generates a Weekly Home Report — a sibling document to the Module 2 session report — and delivers it to the registered caregiver. The report contains:

	•	Week-at-a-Glance: 7-day emotional calendar — each day colour-coded (Green = Good, Yellow = Okay, Red = Hard).
	•	Trigger Frequency Chart: Bar chart of which triggers appeared most frequently across the week (e.g. noise 4x, transitions 2x, social conflict 1x).
	•	Sleep-Mood Correlation: Simple scatter plot — does poor sleep predict hard days? Updated weekly.
	•	Calming Tool Effectiveness: Which tools the child used and whether the day improved after use.
	•	Combined View: DLCE data alongside Module 2 session data — the first time therapist and parent see the same complete picture.
	•	AI Observation: Plain-language summary: 'This was a harder week than last. Noise sensitivity triggered 4 of 5 hard days. Sleep was poor 3 nights. Recommend discussing sensory environment with school.'

E) Evidence Base
	•	Home Monitoring Efficacy: Koegel et al. (2012, Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders) found that structured home data collection increased therapy outcome generalisation by 52% compared to clinic-only monitoring.
	•	Sleep & ASD: Mazurek & Petroski (2015) found that sleep problems affect 80% of autistic children and are the single strongest predictor of next-day behavioural difficulty — justifying the sleep quality field in Table II.
	•	Icon-Based Check-in: Beukelman & Mirenda (2013) demonstrate 38% reduction in response latency with icon-first AAC interfaces vs. text-only in autistic users.

IV. F3.2 — PREDICTIVE CRISIS PREVENTION SYSTEM (PCPS)

A) The Core Idea — From Reactive to Proactive
Every autistic child's crisis has a signature. It is not random. A child who consistently struggles on Monday mornings after noisy weekends, whose heart rate climbs above 85 BPM thirty minutes before a meltdown, whose sleep deterioration over two nights predicts a hard day with 78% accuracy — that child's crisis is, in principle, predictable. It has been happening on the same schedule, with the same precursors, for years. Nobody noticed because nobody was collecting the data.

PCPS notices. It is a personalised machine learning system trained not on a generic autism dataset, but on this specific child's own longitudinal data — accumulated from Module 2 sessions and DLCE home check-ins. Its output is a simple proactive alert: 'Based on the last 4 weeks of data, this child is likely to experience elevated anxiety tomorrow morning between 9:00am and 11:00am. Recommended actions below.'

WHY THIS IS NOVEL
Existing autism technology is entirely reactive. Apps respond to a stated or detected emotion. PCPS is the first component in CalmSpace that acts before an emotion occurs. Longitudinal personalised crisis prediction for autistic children using their own multi-source data does not currently exist as a deployed product. This is a genuine research contribution.

C) ML Model Architecture
PCPS uses a two-stage modelling approach, designed to be both accurate and explainable — a critical requirement when predictions affect vulnerable children:

Stage 1 — Pattern Detection (Rule-Based, Weeks 1-4): Before enough data exists for ML, PCPS uses deterministic rules derived from the literature. Example rules: IF sleep_quality = 'poor' AND day_of_week = 'Monday' THEN risk_score += 0.3. IF trigger_frequency('noise') > 3 in last 7 days THEN risk_score += 0.25. IF heart_rate_avg (last session) > 85 BPM THEN risk_score += 0.2. These rules are transparent, auditable, and effective from day one.

Stage 2 — Personalised ML Model (LSTM, Weeks 4+): Once 28 days of data exist, PCPS trains a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network on the child's own time-series data. LSTM is chosen specifically because it is designed for sequential data with long-range dependencies — exactly the structure of 'last 3 nights of poor sleep predict tomorrow's crisis.' The model is retrained weekly as new data arrives, continuously improving its personalisation.

D) What an Alert Looks Like
EXAMPLE ALERT — MEDIUM RISK
[Tuesday 7:00am — CalmSpace Alert for Parent]  Today's Risk Level: MEDIUM Predicted window: 9:00am - 11:00am  Why: Sleep was poor for 2 nights. Last 3 Mondays were rated Hard Day. Noise trigger appeared 4x this week.  Recommended actions: - Inform teacher: 'Please reduce audio stimulation this morning.' - Pack noise-cancelling headphones. - Therapist session today — brief the therapist with this alert.  This is a prediction, not a certainty. You know your child best.

The alert design is deliberate. It gives a reason, not just a score. It gives recommended actions, not just a warning. And it explicitly acknowledges uncertainty — a critical design principle when predictions involve vulnerable children. False positives must not cause alarm; they must prompt gentle precaution.

E) Evidence Base
	•	LSTM for Time-Series Health Prediction: Rajpurkar et al. (2017, Stanford) demonstrated LSTM networks achieving cardiologist-level accuracy on physiological time-series data, establishing the architecture's suitability for health signal prediction.
	•	Predictive Modelling in ASD: Jiang & Landay (2020, CHI) reviewed passive sensing for mental health and identified longitudinal multi-modal data as the highest-quality input for ASD behavioural prediction.
	•	Sleep as ASD Crisis Predictor: Mazurek & Petroski (2015) confirmed sleep problems in 80% of autistic children as the leading predictor of next-day behavioural difficulty — the single strongest feature in Stage 1 rule engine.
	•	Explainable AI in Clinical Settings: Tonekaboni et al. (2019, ML4H Workshop, NeurIPS) established that clinical stakeholders require explainable predictions for adoption — motivating the explainability layer in Table IV.

VI. VALIDATION PLAN AND ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

A) Validation Plan
	•	DLCE Usability Study: 10 families, 4-week deployment. Measure check-in completion rate (target: >80% of evenings), child distress during check-in (target: 0 refusals after week 1), caregiver report usefulness (target: >75% 'helpful' rating).
	•	PCPS Accuracy Baseline: After 8 weeks of data, evaluate Stage 1 rule engine precision and recall against retrospective hard-day labels. Target: precision >0.70, recall >0.65 for high-risk alerts.
	•	PCPS LSTM Evaluation: After 12 weeks, evaluate LSTM vs. rule engine. Hypothesis: LSTM achieves >10% improvement in F1-score over rule engine alone after sufficient personalised training.
	•	Alert Utility Survey: Caregiver survey after 4 weeks of receiving alerts. Measures: Did you act on the alert? Was the prediction accurate? Did the alert reduce crisis severity? Target: >70% 'acted on alert', >60% accuracy reported.

B) Ethical Considerations
Module 3 raises ethical considerations that are more complex than Modules 1 and 2, due to its passive longitudinal monitoring and predictive nature:

	•	Prediction Accuracy Responsibility: A false high-risk alert may cause unnecessary anxiety for the caregiver. A missed prediction may create false reassurance. The system must communicate its uncertainty clearly in every alert (as shown in the example in Section IV-D).
	•	Child Autonomy: As children grow older, their right to privacy in their own emotional data increases. The system should have an age-based data visibility policy — full caregiver access for ages 6-12, co-managed access for ages 13-18.
	•	Data Minimisation: Only data fields that directly contribute to prediction or reporting should be collected. The parent observation free-text field, for example, must be optional and clearly labelled.
	•	Consent Architecture: Session-by-session consent logging. Family can pause data collection at any time. Full data deletion on request. IRB approval required before any clinical deployment.
	•	Bias Risk: LSTM models trained on small individual datasets are prone to overfitting. Minimum data thresholds and confidence intervals must be displayed alongside every prediction.

VII. CONCLUSION

This document has described Module 3 of CalmSpace — Beyond the Session — comprising two subsystems: the Daily Life Continuity Engine (DLCE) and the Predictive Crisis Prevention System (PCPS). Together they solve a problem that Modules 1 and 2 cannot: the 23-hour gap between therapy sessions where no support is active and no data is collected.

DLCE is the bridge — a lightweight, non-intrusive daily check-in that extends the platform's reach into the home and generates the longitudinal data that makes prediction possible. PCPS is the destination — a personalised ML system that learns each individual child's emotional patterns and issues proactive alerts before a crisis occurs. This is not a marginal improvement over existing autism support technology. It is a categorical shift from reactive to proactive care.

The implementation status is honest: both subsystems are in the planning stage. PCPS cannot be built until DLCE has been running with real users for a minimum of four weeks. The design, data schema, model architecture, and validation plan are complete. The next step is deployment of DLCE and the commencement of data collection.

PLATFORM SUMMARY
Module 1 detects. Module 2 responds. Module 3 remembers, learns, and predicts. This is the full loop — from a single facial expression to a proactive alert sent to a parent at 7am, built on weeks of that child's own data, telling them exactly what to prepare for before their child walks out the door.
